# Operational Data & Reporting Case Study — From Fragmented Files to Usable Information

> **Context:** An anonymised case study based on real reporting and administrative workflows in cultural-site operations in Italy. No employer, visitor or commercially sensitive data is included.

## The operating environment

Public-facing cultural operations generate a surprising amount of small operational data: attendance, group visits, revenue information, visitor provenance, staffing records and statistics requested by institutional stakeholders.

The difficulty is rarely collecting a number once. The difficulty is maintaining information over time when several people contribute to different files and the data still needs to be understandable weeks or months later.

## The problem

The reporting environment relied on multiple spreadsheets created for different operational purposes and periods.

That created familiar risks:

- duplicated manual entry;
- inconsistent structures between files;
- unclear fields and handover conventions;
- historical information that could not simply be discarded;
- increased chance of human error;
- too much knowledge about how a file worked living with individual people;
- time spent reconstructing information when statistics were requested.

The constraint was important: **simplification could not come at the cost of losing historical data.**

## My role

I maintain operational information and compile statistics used in day-to-day management and institutional communication. I also started reviewing the underlying workflow itself rather than accepting the existing files as fixed.

The objective became:

> preserve the source data, make individual files easier for colleagues to use, then create a cleaner master workflow that can reduce repetitive compilation.

## Approach

### 1. Preserve before redesigning

The first rule was not to “clean” the system by deleting inconvenient history.

Existing datasets remain separate where they serve distinct purposes. Errors, inconsistent labels and usability problems are addressed before attempting consolidation or automation.

### 2. Understand the human workflow

A technically elegant spreadsheet is useless if the people entering information do not understand it.

I therefore look at:

- what colleagues actually need to enter;
- which fields are repeatedly misunderstood;
- where the same information is entered more than once;
- which calculations can be automated safely;
- what should remain visible for manual verification;
- how a new colleague would understand the file without oral instructions.

### 3. Separate source data from reporting

The longer-term model is to keep reliable source records and derive recurring summaries from them rather than manually rebuilding the same statistics each time.

This creates a clearer distinction between:

**input → validation → aggregation → reporting.**

That structure also makes errors easier to locate.

### 4. Use AI as an analysis assistant, not an authority

I use AI to help inspect structures, identify inconsistencies, propose formulas and documentation, and think through more efficient workflows.

The source records remain authoritative. Changes are reviewed by a human, and the goal is always traceability rather than opaque automation.

### 5. Design for handover

The system should not require its creator to be present forever.

That means clearer field names, fewer unnecessary steps, documented conventions and eventually a short operating guide so colleagues can maintain the workflow themselves.

## Intended operating model

The redesign follows a simple architecture:

**Daily operational input**  
↓  
**Consistent source records**  
↓  
**Validation / error checks**  
↓  
**Automated or semi-automated aggregation**  
↓  
**Monthly and period reporting**  
↓  
**Institutional / management statistics**

Automation is introduced only where the underlying data is sufficiently reliable.

## What changed

Even before full automation, reviewing the workflow as a system creates value: inconsistencies become visible, historical data is protected, responsibilities are clearer and future reporting can be designed around reusable source information instead of repeated manual reconstruction.

The larger lesson is that operational data quality is a process problem as much as a spreadsheet problem. Better reporting starts with making correct data entry easier for the people closest to the work.

## What this demonstrates

This case demonstrates:

- operational reporting;
- spreadsheet and workflow analysis;
- data-quality thinking;
- preservation and reconciliation of historical records;
- process mapping;
- automation judgment;
- documentation and handover design;
- AI-assisted workflow improvement with human review.
